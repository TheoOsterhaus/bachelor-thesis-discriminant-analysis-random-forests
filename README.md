## Abstract / Introduction

Classification is omnipresent in modern society — from deciding whether to grant a credit card, to detecting spam, to diagnosing and preventing medical conditions. The escalating global prevalence of diabetes mellitus poses a significant public health challenge, requiring effective strategies to identify at-risk individuals early on. Early detection enables timely intervention, thereby improving patient outcomes and reducing healthcare burdens.

This thesis addresses a fundamental challenge in statistical research: the inherent difficulty of selecting the most suitable approach for a given empirical problem — a principle famously articulated by Wolpert and Macready (1997) as *"there is no free lunch in statistical learning."*

We compare the performance of two prominent and fundamentally distinct approaches to classification — **Discriminant Analysis** (Linear Discriminant Analysis and Quadratic Discriminant Analysis) and **Random Forests** — on a series of controlled artificial datasets and on a real-world medical dataset. The objective is to identify a suitable model capable of accurately predicting an individual's diabetes status based on readily available physiological and demographic data, while setting an appropriate threshold.

The empirical application uses the **Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset** (US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, 1998).

## Key Findings / Conclusion

This thesis comparatively evaluates **Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)**, **Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA)**, and **Random Forests** on both idealised artificial data and a realistic medical dataset.

- On **linearly separable** data, **LDA** is superior, QDA performs almost equally well, and Random Forests perform least well.
- On **quadratically separable** data, **QDA** significantly outperforms LDA, while Random Forests effectively approximate the nonlinear pattern.
- On **complex, nonlinear** data (Dataset 03), **Random Forests dominate** due to their flexibility in capturing complex patterns, while LDA and QDA reach their limits.
- Across the artificial datasets, Random Forests consistently deliver superior or at least comparable classification results.
- In the **empirical application to the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset**, Random Forests emerged as the most suitable model, offering balanced performance and a high Recall value. A deliberate reduction in the false-negative rate is ethically and practically essential in a clinical setting, where false negatives can have serious health consequences.

> **Take-away:** The careful selection and deliberate calibration of classification models are essential for translating predictive analyses into practical applications — especially in the field of public health.

Other advanced ensemble methods or deep-learning approaches were not explicitly investigated and remain a direction for future work.

## How to Reproduce

1. Clone or download this repository.
2. Open the R scripts in [`rcode/`](./rcode/) in R or RStudio.
3. Install the required R packages (e.g. `MASS`, `randomForest`, `pROC`, `mlbench`, `caret`).
